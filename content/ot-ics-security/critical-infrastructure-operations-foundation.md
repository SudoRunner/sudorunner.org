## Overview

My role at Accesso Services was not a cybersecurity role, but it strongly shaped how I think about cybersecurity today.

I worked in a major Class A commercial office building at 1515 Market Street in Philadelphia. The building was a large multi-tenant environment with significant mechanical, electrical, life-safety, access, and tenant-support responsibilities. The building was heavily occupied during my time there, and daily operations required coordination between engineering, security, tenants, property management, vendors, and contractors.

The work taught me that infrastructure is not abstract.

A pump, air handler, fire panel, access-controlled room, electrical closet, cooling tower, or generator is not just equipment. It is part of a larger operating environment that people depend on.

That perspective is one reason OT/ICS security is meaningful to me. I understand the physical side of operational risk because I worked around it every day.

---

## What Made This Experience OT/ICS-Relevant

I am careful not to overstate this experience. I was not working as an OT security engineer, ICS architect, or industrial control system specialist.

But I was working in a cyber-physical environment.

The building depended on systems that had to be monitored, maintained, accessed, coordinated, and kept available. I interacted with building management systems, controls, sensors, equipment readings, contractors, work-order platforms, access-controlled spaces, and physical infrastructure.

That experience gave me practical exposure to concepts that matter in OT/ICS security:

* availability matters
* physical access matters
* maintenance windows matter
* vendor access matters
* baseline readings matter
* small failures can create operational impact
* documentation and logs matter
* safety and continuity matter
* people and process matter as much as technology

Those lessons translate directly into how I think about OT/ICS and cyber-physical systems today.

## Contractor and Vendor Coordination

A major part of the job involved supporting contractors and vendors.

Examples included:

* coordinating access to restricted areas
* supporting contractors working on building systems
* assisting with infrastructure modernization projects
* helping vendors understand where they needed to work
* maintaining building operations during contractor activity
* escalating issues when specialized work was required

This taught me that technical work often depends on coordination and trust.

A contractor may understand their specialized system, but building engineering understands the environment: where access is needed, which areas are sensitive, what other systems could be affected, and how work may impact tenants.

That lesson applies directly to cybersecurity implementation work and OT/ICS security.

---

## Major Infrastructure Projects

### 5G Antenna Deployment

One of the major projects I supported was the building's 5G antenna deployment.

From an operations perspective, this required coordination between building engineering, contractors, access-controlled areas, infrastructure locations, and ongoing building operations.

The cybersecurity relevance is not that I configured telecommunications equipment.

The relevance is that I supported an infrastructure modernization project inside an active commercial building where access, coordination, physical placement, operational disruption, and vendor activity all mattered.

That experience helped me understand how infrastructure changes can affect real environments.

### Air Handler Replacement

I also supported major air handler replacement work.

Projects like this were significant because air handlers affect tenant comfort, airflow, scheduling, access, contractor coordination, mechanical space availability, and operational continuity.

Large equipment work is not just a maintenance task. It is a planning and coordination problem.

---

## Access and Physical Security Lessons

One of the most serious responsibilities in building operations was access.

Engineering staff had authorized access to areas that most tenants and visitors could not enter, including mechanical spaces, electrical rooms, roof areas, service spaces, and other restricted parts of the building.

I also supported tenant key creation, rekeying, key stamping, and logging processes.

That experience taught me that physical security is not theoretical.

Access control determines who can reach critical areas. Key control, logging, authorization, and escorting are part of operational risk management.

In cybersecurity terms, this experience helped me understand:

* privileged access
* access control
* least privilege
* auditability
* physical security
* third-party access
* operational trust

Those concepts appear again in OT/ICS security, ServiceNow workflows, and enterprise cybersecurity.

## Building Systems and Work Order Operations

The building used work-order and logging systems to manage tenant requests, engineering activity, and equipment readings.

I worked with tenant-facing requests and operational logs involving:

* tenant issues
* equipment readings
* maintenance activities
* follow-up tasks
* recurring checks
* troubleshooting notes
* vendor activity
* escalation items

This reinforced the importance of documentation.

If work is not logged, tracked, or communicated, it becomes difficult to prove what happened or what still needs attention.

That is the same lesson behind good cybersecurity workflows.

## What I Learned

This job taught me responsibility, accountability, and independence.

I was often the person expected to respond first when something in the building was not working. That could mean a tenant issue, a mechanical concern, an access request, a contractor needing support, a piece of equipment behaving abnormally, or a system requiring escalation.

The experience changed how I think about infrastructure. I learned that buildings are systems of systems. HVAC, electrical, life safety, access, elevators, water, controls, vendors, tenants, and operators all depend on each other.

I also learned that operational work requires judgment. Sometimes the issue is obvious. Other times, the situation requires observation, troubleshooting, escalation, communication, and follow-through.

The most important lesson was that infrastructure responsibility is real. When you are responsible for a building, people expect the environment to work. They usually do not see the maintenance, readings, inspections, and coordination behind the scenes. They only notice when something fails.

That shaped how I think about cybersecurity.

A good security program should work the same way: many disciplined processes operating quietly in the background to prevent disruption, reduce risk, and maintain trust.
